## 1. Terminology and Naming

The term **"Art Deco"** is an abbreviation of the French *Arts Décoratifs* (Decorative Arts), derived from the full name of the 1925 exposition: *Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes* (International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts). The style was not originally called "Art Deco" during its heyday — contemporaries referred to it as *Style Moderne*, *Art Moderne*, or *Jazz Moderne*. The term "Art Deco" only gained widespread currency in the 1960s, following the publication of Bevis Hillier's seminal 1968 book *Art Deco of the 20s and 30s*, which triggered a scholarly and popular revival of interest in the style.

The French *Société des Artistes Décorateurs* (Society of Decorative Artists), founded in 1901, played a foundational role in nurturing the artists and designers who would later define the Art Deco movement. The society organized annual salons that showcased new work in furniture, textiles, jewelry, glass, and metalwork.

## 2. Origins and Stylistic Influences (1900–1925)

### 2.1 Historical Context

Art Deco emerged in France during the first decades of the 20th century as a deliberate break from the sinuous, organic curves of **Art Nouveau** (which had dominated the 1890s–1900s). Where Art Nouveau celebrated nature's flowing lines (vines, flowers, women's hair), Art Deco championed the machine age's geometric precision, symmetry, and streamlined forms. Several intertwined historical forces shaped its development:

- **Post-World War I Euphoria**: After the devastation of WWI (1914–1918), Europe experienced a cultural explosion. Art Deco's opulence, luxury, and celebration of modernity reflected a collective desire to leave behind the trauma of war and embrace a bright, glamorous future.
- **Economic Boom (Les Années Folles)**: France's *Années Folles* ("Crazy Years," the French equivalent of the Roaring Twenties) saw rapid industrial growth, urbanization, and the rise of consumer culture. Art Deco was the aesthetic of this new age of mass production, advertising, and leisure.
- **International Expositions and Colonial Exhibitions**: World's fairs and colonial exhibitions (Paris 1900, 1925; Marseille 1906; etc.) exposed European audiences to non-Western art forms, fueling the taste for exoticism.

### 2.2 Stylistic Influences

Art Deco was fundamentally **eclectic** and **syncretic**, absorbing and transforming influences from diverse sources:

#### a) Cubism (c. 1907–1920s)

The most significant fine-art influence on Art Deco was Cubism (Picasso, Braque, Gris, Léger). Cubism's fragmentation of forms into geometric planes, its use of angular shapes, and its rejection of traditional perspective provided Art Deco designers with a formal vocabulary of **sharp angles, faceted surfaces, and intersecting planes**. The "Cubist" look in decorative arts — zigzag patterns, chevrons, stepped forms — became a hallmark of early Art Deco.

> **Key figure**: **Fernand Léger** (1881–1955), though primarily a Cubist/Futurist painter, directly influenced Art Deco through his use of bold primary colors, mechanical forms, and dynamic compositions. His 1924 film *Ballet Mécanique* epitomized the machine-age aesthetic Art Deco embraced.

#### b) Fauvism (c. 1905–1910)

The intense, non-naturalistic colors of the Fauves (Matisse, Derain, Vlaminck) — particularly their use of shocking pinks, electric blues, and vibrant greens — liberated Art Deco designers from naturalistic palettes. Art Deco interiors and posters frequently deployed the **bold color contrasts** Fauvism pioneered.

#### c) The Ballets Russes (1909–1929)

Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes was perhaps the single most important catalyst for the Art Deco sensibility. When the company performed in Paris from 1909 onward, its explosive combinations of **vibrant Eastern-inspired costumes** (designed by Léon Bakst), **exotic sets**, and **dynamic choreography** captivated French audiences. Bakst's use of intense colors — crimson, emerald, orange, purple — and his incorporation of Orientalist motifs (harem pants, turbans, arabesques) directly influenced fashion designers (Poiret, Chanel) and interior decorators.

> The Ballets Russes premiered *Scheherazade* (1910) with Bakst's costumes, triggering a Parisian craze for all things "Oriental" — harems, turbans, and vivid color combinations that suffused early Art Deco.

#### d) The Vienna Secession (1897–1905)

The Austrian variant of Art Nouveau, led by **Gustav Klimt**, **Josef Hoffmann**, and **Koloman Moser**, was notably more geometric than its French counterpart. The Secessionists' preference for **rectilinear forms, grids, and checkerboard patterns** — particularly in the work of Hoffmann and the Wiener Werkstätte (Vienna Workshops, founded 1903) — directly anticipated Art Deco's geometric rigor. Hoffmann's Palais Stoclet in Brussels (1905–1911), with its marble-clad facades and geometric interiors, was a prototype for Art Deco's luxurious modernism.

#### e) Ancient Egyptian Art (Post-1922)

The discovery of **Tutankhamun's tomb** by Howard Carter in November 1922 triggered "Egyptomania" across the West. Egyptian motifs — **lotus columns, scarabs, winged sun discs, stylized pharaonic figures, stepped pyramids (ziggurats), and geometric friezes** — flooded into Art Deco design across all media. This influence was most visible in:

- **Architecture**: Cinema facades (the "Egyptian Theatre" style), stepped skyscraper crowns
- **Jewelry**: Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels produced Egyptian-revival bracelets, brooches, and necklaces using platinum, diamonds, sapphires, emeralds, and onyx
- **Furniture**: Ruhlmann and others incorporated lotus and papyrus motifs into marquetry
- **Graphic Arts**: Egyptian-inspired typography and decorative borders

Egyptomania was not new (Napoleon's Egyptian campaign in 1798 had sparked an earlier wave), but the 1920s version was distinctively filtered through Art Deco's geometric sensibility.

#### f) Aztec and Mayan Art

Pre-Columbian art from Mesoamerica — particularly the **stepped pyramid forms, geometric stone carvings, and stylized animal motifs** of Aztec and Mayan architecture — impressed Art Deco architects and designers. The **ziggurat or stepped-pyramid silhouette** became one of Art Deco's most recognizable signatures, used in skyscraper crowns, furniture profiles, and decorative screens.

#### g) African Art

African sculpture and masks, which had already influenced Cubism, also entered the Art Deco vocabulary. The stylized, abstract forms of African art informed the **geometric simplification of human figures** in Art Deco decorative panels, jewelry, and graphic design. The "black jazz" craze — epitomized by Josephine Baker's performances at the *Revue Nègre* (1925) — brought African-inspired aesthetics into fashion, poster art, and interior design.

#### h) East Asian Art (Chinese and Japanese)

Chinese porcelain, lacquerware, and Japanese woodblock prints (ukiyo-e) had already influenced Western art since the 19th-century Japonisme craze. Art Deco designers inherited this legacy: **lacquer techniques**, **asymmetric compositions** (borrowed from Japanese prints), **chinoiserie motifs**, and **exotic woods** (ebony, macassar) from East Asia were integrated into Art Deco furniture and decorative objects.

#### i) Persian and Indian Art

The intricate floral and geometric patterns of Persian carpets and miniatures, as well as the rich color palette of Indian textiles (particularly those from the Mughal tradition), contributed to Art Deco's ornamental vocabulary. **Paisley patterns**, **arabesques**, and the use of **precious stones** in decorative inlay work reflected these influences.

#### j) The Machine Age and Modern Technology

Art Deco was profoundly shaped by its celebration of **speed, technology, and the machine**. The automobile, the ocean liner, the locomotive, and the airplane were recurring motifs. The **streamlined aesthetic** — aerodynamic curves, smooth surfaces, horizontal speed lines — became increasingly dominant in the 1930s, evolving into Streamline Moderne (see §4.7).

#### k) Bauhaus (1919–1933)

While Bauhaus and Art Deco are often contrasted (Bauhaus = functionalist, anti-ornament; Art Deco = decorative, luxury-oriented), they shared roots in the **rejection of historical revivalism** and the embrace of **modern materials** (glass, steel, concrete). Le Corbusier's *Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau* at the 1925 Exposition — a stark, functionalist cube — stood in deliberate opposition to the ornate Deco pavilions. Yet both movements participated in the same project of defining modernity. Some designers (e.g., **Robert Mallet-Stevens**, **Pierre Chareau**) blurred the line between the two.

## 3. The 1925 Paris Exposition: Landmark Debut

The **Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes** (April–October 1925) was the event that gave Art Deco its name and launched it onto the world stage.

- **Location**: Paris, along the Seine between the Esplanade des Invalides and the Grand Palais, and across both banks
- **Size**: 55 acres; over 15,000 exhibitors from 20+ countries
- **Total visitors**: Approximately 16 million
- **Criteria**: Exhibits had to be "modern" — no historical pastiches were permitted. This rule explicitly excluded traditionalist and revivalist works, forcing designers to innovate.

### 3.1 Key Pavilions

#### The Hôtel du Collectionneur (Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann)

The undisputed centerpiece of the Exposition was **Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann's** *Hôtel du Collectionneur* (House of a Collector), a palatial pavilion that epitomized Art Deco luxury. The grand salon featured:

- Ruhlmann's signature furniture in exotic woods (macassar ebony, amboyna burl, ivory inlays)
- A vast tapestry by **Léon Voguet**
- Sculptural bronze work by **Alfred Janniot**
- Silver by **Jean Puiforcat**
- A sumptuous color scheme of beige, gold, and deep red

The pavilion presented a vision of the modern Patrician home — opulent, sophisticated, and impeccably crafted. It was the most photographed and discussed exhibit at the fair.

#### The Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau (Le Corbusier)

In deliberate contrast, **Le Corbusier** (Charles-Édouard Jeanneret, 1887–1965) and **Pierre Jeanneret** erected their *Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau* — a stark, white, cubic structure with **ribbon windows, a roof garden, and minimalist interiors**. It was a prototype for mass-produced housing, furnished with industrial furniture (Thonet bentwood chairs, tubular steel). Le Corbusier famously proclaimed that "a house is a machine for living in." The pavilion was so controversial that the exhibition organizers tried to hide it behind a fence. This tension — between luxury craft (Ruhlmann) and functionalist mass production (Le Corbusier) — defined the fault lines of early modern design.

#### Other Notable Pavilions

- **Pavillon de la Société des Artistes Décorateurs**: Showcased suites by the leading decorators of the day: **Süe et Mare**, **Paul Follot**, **Maurice Dufrêne**, and others
- **Grand Palais**: The central hall (the *Cour d'Honneur*) hosted monumental decorative works
- **Pavillon de l'U.R.S.S. (USSR)**: Designed by **Konstantin Melnikov**, an avant-garde glass-and-steel structure
- **Pavillon du Collectionneur**: Not to be confused with Ruhlmann's pavilion — the couturier **Jacques Doucet's** collection of Cubist and Deco art was displayed
- **Pavillon de l'Élégance**: Fashion section featuring mannequins by **Paul Poiret**, **Jeanne Lanvin**, **Madeleine Vionnet**

### 3.2 International Participation and Impact

The Exposition featured dedicated pavilions from Britain, Italy, Denmark, Sweden, Japan, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and other nations. The United States famously **declined to participate** because Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover judged that "there were no modern American decorative arts worthy of exhibition" — a decision that galvanized American designers to catch up.

The Exposition's international jury awarded Grand Prix, gold, silver, and bronze medals that established a canon of Art Deco masters. The event effectively set the stylistic agenda for the next decade.

## 4. The Golden Age (1925–1935)

The decade following the 1925 Exposition saw Art Deco flourish across every domain of design. This was the period of the **Chrysler Building** (1930), **Cassandre's posters**, **Chanel's little black dress**, and **Ruhlmann's masterwork furniture**. The style became synonymous with modernity, luxury, and the Jazz Age.

### 4.1 Architecture

Art Deco architecture was a global phenomenon, expressed most dramatically in the **skyscraper** but also in cinemas, theaters, department stores, factories, gas stations, and private homes.

#### Key Characteristics (Architecture)

- **Stepped or ziggurat forms**: Setback massing, inspired by both Egyptian pyramids and zoning laws (New York's 1916 Zoning Resolution required setbacks to allow light to reach the street)
- **Vertical emphasis**: Piers and pilasters rising the full height of buildings, emphasizing upward thrust
- **Zigzag and chevron patterns**: The "lightning bolt" motif, used in brickwork, terra-cotta, and metal spandrels
- **Sunburst motifs**: Radiating arches and fan patterns at entrances and crowning elements
- **Ornamental metalwork**: Elaborate bronze and wrought-iron grilles, doors, elevator doors
- **Polychrome decoration**: Glazed terra-cotta in colors — gold, turquoise, black, red, cream
- **Exotic materials**: Marble, polished granite, stainless steel, aluminum, Murano glass

#### Major Architectural Figures and Works

##### French Architects

**Robert Mallet-Stevens** (1886–1945) — The leading French Art Deco architect. His **Rue Mallet-Stevens** in Paris (1926–1927) was a street of Cubist-inspired houses for artists and patrons. His **Villa Noailles** in Hyères (1923–1928) — a geometric white concrete villa with rooftop terraces and a Cubist garden — was a quintessential Art Deco residence.

**Michel Roux-Spitz** (1888–1957) — Known for his white, streamlined Parisian apartment buildings with horizontal ribbon windows (anticipating Le Corbusier's *fenêtres en longueur*).

**Pierre Chareau** (1883–1950) — Architect/designer of the **Maison de Verre** (House of Glass, 1932) in Paris, a masterpiece of glass-block construction that fused Art Deco transparency with functionalist planning.

**Henri Sauvage** (1873–1932) — Pioneer of stepped-back architecture in Paris (e.g., the **Immeuble à Gradins**, Rue des Amiraux, 1925), using tiered terraces that anticipated the American skyscraper setback.

##### American Architects

**William Van Alen** (1883–1954) — Designer of the **Chrysler Building** (1928–1930), the quintessential Art Deco skyscraper. Van Alen studied at the Beaux-Arts in Paris before working in New York. His Chrysler Building features:

- A stainless-steel **sunburst crown** of seven concentric, triangular arches
- **"Hood ornaments"** — giant replicas of Chrysler radiator caps at the 31st floor
- **Eagle gargoyles** at the 61st floor, modeled after Chrysler's hood ornament
- A spectacular lobby faced with **African Rouge Royal marble**, **yellow Siena marble**, and an Egyptian-inspired geometric ceiling mural

The building was briefly the world's tallest (1,046 ft / 319 m) until the Empire State Building surpassed it in 1931.

**Raymond Hood** (1881–1934) — Architect of **Rockefeller Center** (1930–1939) and the **American Radiator Building** (1924) in New York. Rockefeller Center, a 14-building complex in midtown Manhattan, is the world's largest Art Deco development. Key features:

- **Radio City Music Hall** (1932) — interiors by **Donald Deskey** in a spectacular Deco mode with gold leaf, mirrors, and neon
- The **GE Building** (formerly RCA Building) — a slender limestone slab with vertical piers
- **Sculptural decoration** by **Paul Manship** (including *Prometheus* in the sunken plaza)
- The complex unified Art Deco design across architecture, sculpture, and interior design

**Ralph Thomas Walker** (1889–1973) — Known for the **Barclay-Vesey Building** (1926) and the **New York Telephone Company Building**, often considered the first Art Deco skyscraper, with elaborate brickwork and Gothic-inspired Deco ornament.

##### Other Notable Architects

**Eliel Saarinen** (1873–1950) — Finnish-American architect whose 1922 design for the Chicago Tribune Tower competition (second prize) popularized the stepped, vertical skyscraper form that became synonymous with Art Deco. Saarinen's design, though unbuilt, was enormously influential. He later designed the **Cranbrook Academy of Art** (1926–1942) in Michigan.

**Josep Puig i Cadafalch** — In Spain, the **Palau Nacional** (Barcelona, 1929) for the 1929 International Exposition.

**Henri Prost**, **Antoine Laharge**, and others who designed French colonial buildings in North Africa and Indochina in a "Colonial Deco" style.

### 4.2 Graphic Design and Posters

Art Deco graphic design was characterized by bold, simplified forms, geometric typography, dynamic compositions, and strong color contrasts. The poster was the medium par excellence, as advertising flourished in the consumer economy of the 1920s.

#### Key Characteristics (Graphic Design)

- **Geometric typefaces**: Bold, sans-serif, often compressed or elongated letterforms (e.g., Cassandre's **Bifur** typeface, 1929; **A.M. Cassandre**'s **Peignot**, 1937)
- **Diagonal and dynamic compositions**: Objects tilted at dramatic angles to convey speed and energy
- **Bold color contrasts**: Black with red, gold, or white; deep blues with orange; noir et blanc (black and white) for elegance
- **Simplified, stylized forms**: Reduction of figures to geometric shapes, strong outlines
- **Airbrushed gradients**: Smooth tonal transitions (fade, ombré effects)
- **Symmetry and architectural framing**: Pillars, stepped forms, arches used as compositional devices
- **Surreal spatial depth**: Distorted perspectives, dramatic foreshortening

#### Major Graphic Artists

**Erté — Romain de Tirtoff** (1892–1990)

- Russian-born, moved to Paris 1912; studied under and collaborated with Paul Poiret
- Signed a contract with **Harper's Bazaar** in 1915, designing over **240 covers** over 22 years
- Known for **sinuous, elegant female figures** in elaborate costumes and jeweled settings
- Worked across fashion illustration, costume design (for film, theater, opera), set design, jewelry, and interior decoration
- His signature style: figures with **elongated limbs**, **androgynous elegance**, **lavish drapery and fur**, **rich jewel tones**
- Key works: *The Symphony in Black* (lithograph, 1930s), *Dieu Bleu* costume designs for Ballets Russes, Harper's Bazaar covers
- Called "the father of Art Deco" by many

**A.M. Cassandre — Adolphe Jean-Marie Mouron** (1901–1968)

- Born in Kharkiv (then Russian Empire) to French parents; studied at École des Beaux-Arts in Paris
- The most influential poster artist of the Art Deco era
- Pioneered a **machine-age poster style** that reduced imagery to geometric essentials
- His philosophy: "A poster is made to be noticed by people who have not sought it out. It must elbow its way in."
- Key Works:
  - *Au Bûcheron* (1923) — his breakthrough, for a furniture store
  - *Nord Express* (1927) — a speeding train seen in extreme perspective, with tracks converging to a vanishing point; telegraph wires echoing its horizontal rush
  - *L'Intransigeant* (1925) — a shouting head whose voice transforms into radiating lines
  - *Normandie* (1935) — the majestic ocean liner seen from below, bow-first, dominating the composition, with dramatic clouds and a three-masted silhouette in the background
  - *Dubonnet* (1932) — a three-panel sequential poster showing a man drinking Dubonnet, his posture collapsing progressively, a witty narrative
- Also designed typefaces: **Bifur** (1929), **Acier** (1930), **Peignot** (1937)
- His work influenced Swiss typographic design and later pop art

**Paul Colin** (1892–1985)

- The most prolific poster artist of the era, producing over **1,900 posters**
- Best known for his posters for the **Revue Nègre** (1925), featuring **Josephine Baker**
- His *Bal Nègre* poster (1927) and *Le Tumulte Noir* series captured the Jazz Age's love of African-American music and dance
- He worked in theater set design, costume design, and book illustration
- His style combined **bold silhouettes, rhythmic compositions, and intense color** with a sense of movement and musicality

**Georges Barbier** (1882–1932)

- A leading fashion illustrator and costume designer; contributed to the *Almanach des Arts Décoratifs* and *La Gazette du Bon Ton*
- His elegant, stylized figures in antique or exotic costumes epitomized the **high-fashion Deco** look
- Designed costumes for the Ballets Russes and theater

**Jean Carlu** (1900–1997)

- Known for his posters for cinema (including films by Jean Renoir) and public service (WWII propaganda)
- Created the famous *America's Answer! Production* poster (1941) for US wartime production

**Charles Gesmar** (1900–1928)

- Prodigy; designed posters for Mistinguett and the Casino de Paris
- Known for his theatrical, energetic compositions

### 4.3 Furniture and Interior Design

Art Deco furniture combined **luxurious materials, exquisite craftsmanship, and geometric forms**. It was the domain of the *ensemblier* (decorator/designer who conceived the total interior), a profession that flourished in 1920s Paris.

#### Key Characteristics (Furniture and Interiors)

- **Exotic woods**: Macassar ebony, amboyna burl, palissander (rosewood), zebrano, mahogany
- **Precious inlays**: Ivory, mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell, silver, gold, shagreen (stingray skin)
- **Lacquer**: High-gloss Asian lacquer in black, red, and gold; applied to furniture, screens, and panels
- **Geometric marquetry**: Stylized floral and geometric patterns in contrasting veneers
- **Streamlined silhouettes**: Curved, aerodynamic shapes in later furniture
- **Sculptural metal mounts**: Ormolu (gilt bronze), wrought iron, chromed steel
- **Upholstery**: Silk, velvet, leather, often in bold geometric patterns
- **Function**: Cabinets, desks, commodes, dining tables, bed frames, screens, lighting fixtures

#### Major Furniture Designers

**Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann** (1879–1933)

- The preeminent French furniture designer of the Art Deco era
- Son of a decorating contractor; founded his own firm in 1910, partnering with **Pierre Laurent**
- Believed in **luxury as a necessity** — his furniture was handmade by master craftsmen, using only the rarest materials
- His pavilion at the 1925 Exposition, the *Hôtel du Collectionneur*, was the event's centerpiece and established his international reputation
- Key works:
  - *Cabinet* (1916) — simple geometric form in macassar ebony with ivory inlay
  - *Chiffonnier* (1923) — a tall cabinet with subtle curves, veneered in amboyna burl
  - *Table à jeu* (gaming table, c. 1923) — with ivory and precious wood marquetry
  - *Grand bureau de dame* (1925) — desk from the Hôtel du Collectionneur, in macassar ebony and ivory
- His apprentices included **Jean-Michel Frank** and **Gilles Derain**
- Died in 1933, but his atelier continued under Laurent until the 1950s

**Louis Süe (1875–1968) and André Mare (1885–1932) — Süe et Mare**

- Partnership formed in 1919; operated a workshop and boutique on the Faubourg Saint-Honoré
- Founded the **Compagnie des Arts Français** (1919) to revive French decorative arts after WWI
- Their style: **monumental, architectonic, with rich color** — marquetry incorporating flowers, ribbons, musical instruments
- Strongly influenced by the Louis-Philippe and Empire styles, reinterpreted through a modern geometric lens
- Designed the **Pavillon du Musée d'Art Contemporain** at the 1925 Exposition
- Produced complete interiors for ocean liners (including the *Normandie*), embassies, and grand hotels

**Jean-Michel Frank** (1895–1941)

- A disciple of Ruhlmann, known for a more **austere, minimalist** version of Art Deco
- Used **parchment (vellum), shagreen, straw marquetry (marqueterie de paille), and plaster** as luxury materials
- Worked with décorateur **Adolphe Chanaux**; collaborated with artists **Salvador Dalí** and **Christian Bérard**
- Designed interiors for the fashion house **Lanvin**, the **Villa Noailles**, and wealthy patrons
- His pared-back elegance — streamlined sofas, simple geometric tables, neutral tones — anticipated mid-century modernism

**Paul T. Frankl** (1886–1958)

- Viennese-born, emigrated to the US in 1914
- Created the **"Skyscraper" furniture** line (1920s) — bookcases, cabinets, and desks that mimicked the stepped silhouette of American skyscrapers, in California redwood and black lacquer with silver leaf
- His Manhattan gallery on East 48th Street (1922) was a center for avant-garde design
- Published *New Dimensions* (1928), arguing for an American design language based on skyscrapers, speed, and modernity

**Jules Leleu** (1883–1961) — Haute-quality furniture with restrained elegance; supplied the French ocean liner *Normandie*.

**Jacques-Émile Ruhlmann's atelier** (continued by others after 1933).

**Donald Deskey** (1894–1989) — American interior/industrial designer; famous for the interiors of **Radio City Music Hall** (1932), with its lavish use of mirrors, metal, Bakelite, and murals.

### 4.4 Fashion

Art Deco fashion liberated the female body from the corseted, S-curve silhouette of the Belle Époque and replaced it with **straight, tubular, geometric lines** that emphasized movement, freedom, and modernity. The **flapper** — with her bobbed hair, dropped waist, short skirt, and cloche hat — was Art Deco's human embodiment.

#### Key Fashion Figures

**Paul Poiret** (1879–1944)

- Called "the King of Fashion" in pre-WWI Paris
- Abolished the corset (c. 1906), introducing a high-waisted, columnar silhouette inspired by Greek and Near Eastern dress
- His **Directoire and Orientalist styles** — lampshade tunics, harem pants, turbans — created the vogue for exoticism that Art Deco absorbed
- Collaborated with artist **Paul Iribe** to publish *Les Robes de Paul Poiret* (1908), a book of fashion illustrations considered a landmark of Art Deco graphic art
- After WWI, his elaborate, theatrical style fell out of favor as Chanel's simpler modernism took over (the famous exchange: Poiret asked Chanel, "For whom do you mourn?" and she replied, "For you, monsieur.")
- His 1925 pavilion at the Exposition, *Le Jardin des Délices*, featured costumed mannequins in three gardens

**Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel** (1883–1971)

- The defining fashion figure of the Art Deco era
- Replaced Poiret's orientalism with a **modernist, androgynous simplicity**
- Introduced **jersey fabric** (previously used for underwear) for women's suits (c. 1916) — comfortable, cheap, and modern
- Popularized the **little black dress** (c. 1926 — *Vogue* called it "the little black dress that all the world will wear"), the **cardigan jacket**, the **quilted handbag**, and **costume jewelry** (pearls worn in multiples)
- Her signature: **black, beige, white, navy**; clean lines; no frills; functional elegance
- Her Chanel No. 5 perfume bottle (1921) — stark, rectangular, label-less — was itself an Art Deco object
- Collaborated with the Ballets Russes and designed costumes for films

**Madeleine Vionnet** (1876–1975)

- The **architect of dressmaking**; pioneered the **bias cut** (cutting fabric on the diagonal), which allowed dresses to drape fluidly over the body
- Her geometric approach to pattern-making — cutting fabric into precise geometric shapes that assembled into three-dimensional forms — was a direct analogue to Cubism in fashion
- Her gowns were known as **"masterpieces of mathematical construction"**
- Influenced by classical Greek drapery, she created the **goddess dress**, with its long, clinging lines

**Jeanne Lanvin** (1867–1946)

- One of the most influential Parisian couturiers; her **robes de style** (with full, puffed skirts and fitted bodices) maintained a feminine, romantic counterpoint to Chanel's androgyny
- Her Art Deco interiors were designed by the architect **Armand-Albert Rateau**; her house on Rue Barbet-de-Jouy featured a Deco dining room with Jean Dunand lacquer panels

**Elsa Schiaparelli** (1890–1973)

- Entered fashion in the late 1920s; brought **Surrealist wit** and **shocking pink** to Art Deco fashion
- Collaborated with artists Salvador Dalí, Jean Cocteau, and Meret Oppenheim
- Known for her innovative textures (cellophane, plastics, paper dress) and whimsical motifs (lobster dress, shoe hat, skeleton dress)

### 4.5 Jewelry and Decorative Arts

Art Deco jewelry represents the pinnacle of the style's **luxury, geometric precision, and exoticism**. The discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb (1922) and the opening up of new sources of precious stones drove both style and technique.

#### Key Characteristics (Jewelry)

- **Platinum** as the dominant metal (white, strong, capable of intricate settings)
- **Geometric and architectural** designs: rectangles, step-cuts, triangles, chevrons
- **Egyptian revival**: scarabs, sphinxes, lotus blossoms, winged discs, hieroglyphics
- **Neoclassical and Persian** motifs: garlands, baskets, arabesques, paisley
- **Calibré-cut gemstones**: sapphires, rubies, emeralds cut to exact shapes and set flush together in mosaic patterns (tutti frutti style by Cartier)
- **Diamonds** in baguette and emerald cuts (the step-cut allowed for the clean geometric lines Art Deco favored)
- **Onyx, coral, jade, lapis lazuli** as colored accents
- **Enamel** in bold black, white, and red
- **Cocktail rings**, sautoir necklaces, long earrings, bangle bracelets, vanity cases

#### Major Jewelry Houses

**Cartier** (founded 1847) — The most important Art Deco jeweler. Under **Louis Cartier** and **Jacques Cartier**, the house pioneered **Egyptian Revival** designs after 1922. The **"Tutti Frutti"** style (carved rubies, sapphires, emeralds set in platinum) was a signature. Cartier also created **mystery clocks** (with invisible mechanisms) and **vanity cases** in platinum, enamel, and precious stones.

**Van Cleef & Arpels** (founded 1906) — Invented the **"mystery setting" (serti invisible)** in 1933, where gemstones are set without visible prongs, creating a seamless surface of color. Their distinctive **minaudière** (a compact vanity case, 1934) was an Art Deco icon. Egyptian and Persian motifs were recurring themes.

**Boucheron**, **Mauboussin**, **Fouquet**, **Lacloche Frères**, and **Georges Fouquet** were also significant Art Deco jewelry houses.

**René Lalique** (1860–1945) — Though primarily associated with Art Nouveau, Lalique's later work in **glass** defined Art Deco decorative arts. He produced **glass vases, car mascots, jewelry, perfume bottles, and architectural glass panels** with frosted, molded, and enameled motifs — nudes, flowers, animals, geometric patterns. His iconic *bacchantes* vase (1927) and the **Hirondelles** (swallows) pattern were mass-produced luxury items.

**Jean Puiforcat** (1897–1945) — The leading Art Deco silversmith. His **geometric, Cubist-inspired silverware** — tea services, centerpieces, flatware — in silver, gold, and lapis lazuli, combined machine-like precision with aristocratic elegance.

### 4.6 Painting and Sculpture

While Art Deco was primarily a design/decorative arts movement, certain painters and sculptors are closely associated with it:

**Tamara de Lempicka** (1898–1980)

- Polish-born, Russian-trained, active in Paris
- The **quintessential Art Deco painter** — the only easel painter of the movement
- Her style: **Cubist-derived geometry** + **neo-classical figure modeling** + **bold, sensual color**
- Subjects: glamorous (often androgynous) women, nudes, portraits of the elite
- Her compositions emphasize **smooth, polished surfaces**, **twisting, monumental figures**, and **compressed spatial depth**
- Key works:
  - *Autoportrait (Tamara in a Green Bugatti)* (1929) — for the cover of *Die Dame* magazine; herself as a modern, liberated woman, helmeted and driving
  - *La Belle Rafaëla* (1927) — a monumental nude with a Cubist backdrop
  - *Portrait de Marjorie Ferry* (1932) — sold at Christie's in 2020 for £16.38 million, a record for the artist
  - *Les Deux Amies* (1923) — two female nudes in a dynamic, twisted composition

**Jean Dupas** (1882–1964) — French painter and decorative artist; designed the iconic mural *Les Perruches* (The Parakeets) for the *Normandie* ocean liner.

**Paul Manship** (1885–1966) — American sculptor; his gilded bronze *Prometheus* (1934) at Rockefeller Center's sunken plaza is an Art Deco icon.

**Alfred Janniot** (1889–1969) — French sculptor; created the monumental bas-relief *Les Cinque Parties du Monde* (1931) for the Palais de la Porte Dorée, Paris.

### 4.7 Industrial Design and Streamline Moderne

In the 1930s, Art Deco evolved into **Streamline Moderne** (also called *Art Moderne* or *Depression Modern*), a more restrained, aerodynamic variant that emphasized **smooth curves, horizontal lines, and functional simplicity**. This shift was driven by:

- The **Great Depression** (1929 onward), which made luxury Art Deco seem insensitive
- Advances in **industrial design** as a profession
- **Aerodynamic theory**, which inspired the teardrop and bullet forms
- The need for **mass production** — Streamline Moderne was easier to produce cheaply

#### Key Industrial Designers

**Raymond Loewy** (1893–1986)

- French-born, emigrated to the US in 1919; became "the father of industrial design"
- His principle: **"MAYA"** — Most Advanced, Yet Acceptable — design that pushed forward without alienating consumers
- Key works:
  - **Gestetner duplicating machine** (1929) — his first major redesign; streamlined with a shell-like cover, cutting parts by 75%
  - **Coldspot refrigerator** (1934) for Sears — white enamel, smooth curves, hidden hardware
  - **GG-1 locomotive** (1934) for Pennsylvania Railroad — sleek, streamlined, welded steel
  - **Lucky Strike cigarette package** (1942) — the iconic green circle on white background
  - **Studebaker Champion** (1947) — post-war streamlined car
  - **Frigidaire**, **Schick razors**, **Coca-Cola fountain dispensers**, interior of the **Air Force One** for President Kennedy

**Norman Bel Geddes** (1893–1958)

- Originally a stage designer (sets for Max Reinhardt), turned industrial designer
- His book *Horizons* (1932) was the theoretical manifesto of Streamline Moderne
- Key works:
  - **Futurama** (1939–40 New York World's Fair) — sponsored by General Motors; an enormous model of the "World of Tomorrow," viewed by visitors from moving chairs, showing superhighways and automated cities; one of the most popular exhibits of all time
  - **Patriot radio** (1940) for Emerson
  - **Aircraft carrier** designs for the US Navy
  - **Teardrop-shaped automobiles and aircraft** (concept designs)

**Walter Dorwin Teague** (1883–1960)

- Began as a graphic designer; became one of America's first independent industrial designers
- Known for his collaborations with **Kodak** and **Ford**
- Key works:
  - **Kodak Baby Brownie camera** (1934) — Bakelite, streamlined; sold millions
  - **Texaco service stations** (1934–1937) — streamlined, white porcelain enamel, the "Texaco Green" uniform
  - **Marmon 16** automobile concept (1931)
  - **Ford Building** at the 1939 New York World's Fair — a futuristic rotunda
  - **Steuben glassware** for Corning (1930s)

**Henry Dreyfuss** (1904–1972)

- Stage designer turned industrial designer; emphasized **ergonomics and human-centered design**
- Key works:
  - **20th Century Limited** train (1938) for the New York Central Railroad — the iconic streamlined train
  - **Model 302 telephone** (1937) for Bell Labs — the first modern desk phone
  - **John Deere tractors** (1937–) — the streamlined "Model A"
  - **Honeywell thermostat** — the classic round design

**Donald Deskey** (1894–1989) — Radio City Music Hall interiors; designed packaging for Procter & Gamble; pioneered the profession of industrial design.

## 6. Global Spread

Art Deco was arguably **the first truly international style** of the 20th century. Its spread was facilitated by multiple channels:

### 6.1 Channels of Transmission

#### a) International Expositions

After the 1925 Paris Exposition, subsequent world's fairs propagated Art Deco globally:
- **1929 Barcelona International Exposition**: Inspired the Montjuïc neighborhood and the Palau Nacional
- **1931 Paris Colonial Exposition**: Showcased Colonial Deco architecture, combining European Deco with local traditions
- **1933–34 Century of Progress Exposition** (Chicago): Showcased American industrial design and streamline architecture
- **1937 Paris Exposition Internationale des Arts et Techniques**: Marked the transition from Art Deco to more sober modernism
- **1939–40 New York World's Fair**: The apotheosis of Streamline Moderne

#### b) Cinema and Hollywood

Hollywood was a crucial vector for Art Deco's global reach:
- Film sets (especially MGM, Paramount, RKO musicals) featured lavish Art Deco interiors
- Stars like **Joan Crawford**, **Greta Garbo**, **Marlene Dietrich** were photographed in Art Deco settings, making the style aspirational worldwide
- **Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers** films (e.g., *Top Hat*, 1935) cemented Deco glamour
- Cinema architecture itself became a carrier of the style — **movie palaces** from Oslo to Buenos Aires to Mumbai were built in Art Deco
- The film *The Great Gatsby* (1974, directed by Jack Clayton) later sparked a 1970s Deco revival

#### c) Ocean Liners

The great transatlantic liners were **floating showcases** of Art Deco design:
- **SS *Île de France*** (1927) — the first liner to be decorated entirely in Art Deco
- **SS *Normandie*** (1935) — the most spectacular Art Deco interior ever created, with grand dining rooms, smoking rooms, and lounges by Ruhlmann, Leleu, Lalique, Dupas, Dunand, and Puiforcat
- **RMS *Queen Mary*** (1936) — British Streamline Moderne interiors
- **SS *Conte di Savoia*** (1932) — Italian Deco
- These liners carried the style to every continent, inspiring local designers

#### d) Colonial Networks

European colonial powers carried Art Deco to their colonies:
- **French Indochina** (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia): Buildings in Hanoi, Saigon, Phnom Penh, and Vientiane combined Art Deco with local traditions
- **French North Africa** (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia): "Colonial Deco" — white Cubist blocks with Arabic screens and tilework
- **British India**: The **Odeon cinema** in Mumbai (1932) and other buildings; the **Mumbai Art Deco district** (Ocean Drive of the East)
- **Dutch East Indies** (Indonesia): Bandung's Art Deco buildings, including the **Villa Isola** (1933) and the **Savoy Homann hotel**

#### e) Architectural Journals and Travel

- **L'Illustration**, **Vogue**, **Harper's Bazaar**, and architecture magazines (e.g., **Architectural Record**, **L'Architecture d'Aujourd'hui**) spread images of Art Deco worldwide
- Architects and designers traveled and exchanged ideas through exhibitions and the growing network of international design schools

### 6.2 Regional Responses

#### North America
- **United States**: The skyscraper cities — New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, Detroit, Miami — embraced Art Deco most enthusiastically
- **Miami Beach**: The world's largest concentration of Art Deco architecture (over 800 buildings in the **Miami Beach Architectural District**), built during the 1930s real estate boom after the 1926 hurricane. "**Tropical Deco**" — pastel colors, nautical motifs (portholes, waves), Streamline Moderne curves adapted to the subtropical climate

#### Latin America
- **Brazil**: Rio de Janeiro's **Cristo Redentor** statue (1931, designed by Heitor da Silva Costa with Paul Landowski) has Art Deco proportions; the **Edifício Guinle** (1929) in Rio; the **São Paulo Art Deco** buildings
- **Argentina**: Buenos Aires — the **Abasto Market** (1934), the **Teatro Gran Rex** (1937), and the **Racing Club Stadium**
- **Mexico**: The **Palacio de Bellas Artes** (1934, completed by Federico Mariscal) with its curtain of Tiffany glass and Art Deco ornament

#### Europe (Beyond France)
- **Britain**: The **Daily Express Building** (London, 1932, by Ellis & Clarke), **Hoover Building** (Perivale, 1932), **Broadcasting House** (1932), **Odeon cinemas** nationwide
- **Germany**: **Chilehaus** (Hamburg, 1924, by Fritz Höger) — expressive brick Art Deco/Expressionism; **Kaufhaus Schocken** (Stuttgart, 1926, by Erich Mendelsohn)
- **Italy**: The **Stazione di Santa Maria Novella** (Florence, 1934, by Giovanni Michelucci), **Palazzo della Civiltà Italiana** (Rome, 1939, EUR district)
- **Scandinavia**: **Stockholm Public Library** (1928, Gunnar Asplund); **Funkis** style — Swedish Functionalism with Deco elements
- **USSR**: Moscow's Mayakovskaya metro station (1938) — Soviet Art Deco
- **Spain**: **Edificio Capitol** (Madrid, 1933) with its iconic Tío Pepe sign

#### Asia
- **India**: Mumbai has the **second-largest concentration of Art Deco buildings** in the world after Miami (the **Oval Oval Art Deco Precinct**); examples include the **South Court Mansion**, the **New India Assurance Building**, and the **Regal Cinema**
- **Japan**: The **National Diet Building** (Tokyo, 1936), the **Wako Building** (Ginza, 1932), and the **Imperial Hotel** (Frank Lloyd Wright, 1923 — a hybrid of Deco and Wright's Prairie Style)
- **Shanghai**: The **Cathay Hotel (和平饭店/Peace Hotel)** (1929, designed by László Hudec for Victor Sassoon), the **Park Hotel** (1934, also by Hudec, Shanghai's tallest building until 1980s), the **Capitol Theatre** (1928), the **Metropole Hotel** (1933), and countless Deco apartment buildings (e.g., **Grosvenor House**, **Willow Court**) — this is the context directly relevant to Prof. Zhu's research
- **China (other)**: **Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum** (Nanjing, 1929) — China's "National Style" architecture with Deco elements; **Harbin Art Deco** buildings from the Manchurian Railway era

## 7. Late Phase and Decline (1935–1945)

### 7.1 The Shift to Streamline Moderne

By the mid-1930s, the extravagant, luxury-oriented "Zigzag Moderne" of the 1920s gave way to the sleeker, less ornamented **Streamline Moderne**. This was partly:

- **Economic**: The Great Depression (1929–c.1939) made the conspicuous consumption of high Art Deco seem inappropriate and unaffordable
- **Aesthetic**: The machine-age "speed" aesthetic turned toward aerodynamic teardrop forms
- **Ideological**: The rise of European totalitarian regimes (Italian Fascism, German Nazism, Stalinism) appropriated state versions of Classicism and Deco for propaganda, while modernists (Le Corbusier, Gropius, Mies) rejected ornament entirely

### 7.2 Causes of Decline

#### a) World War II (1939–1945)

The most decisive factor in Art Deco's decline was World War II:
- **Wartime austerity**: Rationing of materials (metal, wood, fabric) made luxury production impossible
- **Shift in priorities**: War production replaced consumer goods manufacturing
- **Patriotism and sobriety**: The frivolity, glamour, and excess of 1920s Art Deco seemed tone-deaf in a world of war, death, and deprivation
- **Destruction of buildings**: Many European Art Deco buildings were damaged or destroyed

#### b) The Rise of Modernism and the International Style

After WWII, the dominant design orthodoxy became the **International Style** (the Bauhaus- and Le Corbusier-derived functionalism): **what worked was what was useful; ornament was crime**. The leading voices of post-war architecture and design — **Mies van der Rohe**, **Walter Gropius**, **Le Corbusier**, **the Bauhaus diaspora**, and the **Ulm School of Design** — rejected Art Deco as:

- **Inauthentic**: Ornament was "dishonest" (Adolf Loos argued this as early as 1908 in *Ornament and Crime*)
- **Elitist**: Art Deco's luxury was for the rich; Modernism claimed to serve all
- **Outdated**: Art Deco was associated with the Jazz Age, the Roaring Twenties — a bygone, decadent era

#### c) The Changing of Taste

- Post-war baby boomers associated Art Deco with **their parents' generation** — the speakeasies and flappers of the 1920s — and sought a new aesthetic
- Suburbanization and the post-war housing boom demanded cheaper, more practical design
- The **U.S. Interstate Highway System** and **automobile culture** favored stripped-down, commercial modernism

#### d) Association with Totalitarian Regimes

- In Italy, **Mussolini's Fascist architecture** (the EUR district in Rome) used an imperial, stripped-classical variant of Art Deco
- In Germany, Nazi architecture (Albert Speer's buildings, the Reich Chancellery) used a neo-classical monumentalism that overlapped with Deco's love of grand scale and rich materials
- In the Soviet Union, the **Stalinist Empire style** (the "Seven Sisters" skyscrapers in Moscow) incorporated Deco elements into Socialist Realist monumentalism
- This political association tainted the style for post-war democrats

### 7.3 The Fading (1940–1960)

By 1940, Art Deco was largely out of fashion in the West. The word itself was not even in common usage — people simply called it "modernistic" or "art moderne." Buildings continued to be built in the Streamline Moderne idiom through the 1940s and into the 1950s in some places (especially in Latin America and India), but the energy was gone.

In 1966, the demolition of the **Pere Marquette Hotel** in Peoria, Illinois — a lavish Art Deco structure — symbolized the style's neglect. Many Art Deco buildings were torn down or "modernized" (i.e., stripped of their ornament) during the 1950s and 1960s.

## 8. Revival (1960s–Present)

### 8.1 First Wave: Scholarly Rediscovery (1960s)

The Art Deco revival began in the 1960s, driven by several converging factors:

- **Bevis Hillier's** *Art Deco of the 20s and 30s* (1968) — the book that named the style and argued for its serious study. Hillier organized an exhibition of the same name at the **Minneapolis Institute of Art** (1971) that traveled to the Victoria and Albert Museum, London
- **Pop Art and Op Art** of the 1960s revived interest in bold geometric patterns, bright colors, and commercial imagery — all of which resonated with Art Deco
- **Postmodern architecture** (c. 1970s onward) — architects like **Robert Venturi**, **Philip Johnson**, and **Michael Graves** rejected the austerity of International Style and returned to **ornament, historical reference, color, and symbolism**. Postmodernism's "learning from Las Vegas" (Venturi) and revival of "decorated sheds" opened the door for Art Deco's rehabilitation
- **The nostalgia wave**: Films like *Bonnie and Clyde* (1967), *The Great Gatsby* (1974), and *Chinatown* (1974) fed a wider 1930s nostalgia
- **Fashion**: The 1960s–70s saw a revival of 1920s–30s silhouettes, hairstyles, and accessories

### 8.2 The Miami Beach Preservation Movement (1970s–1980s)

The most visible Art Deco revival was in **Miami Beach, Florida**:

- By the 1970s, Miami Beach's Art Deco district (over 800 hotels and apartment buildings in a one-square-mile area) had fallen into severe disrepair, serving the elderly poor population ("heaven's waiting room")
- **Barbara Baer Capitman** (1920–2003) founded the **Miami Design Preservation League** (MDPL) in 1976, fighting to save the buildings from demolition
- In 1979, the **Miami Beach Architectural District** was listed on the **National Register of Historic Places**
- Architect and designer **Leonard Horowitz** developed the "pastel palette" — the now-iconic colors of Miami's Art Deco (powder pink, seafoam green, baby blue, lavender, coral) — based on historic paint research
- The district's revival was catalyzed by the TV series *Miami Vice* (1984–1989), which set its glamorous, neon-lit aesthetic against Deco backdrops
- Today, the district is the **world's largest concentration of Art Deco architecture** and a UNESCO World Heritage candidate (listed on the Tentative List in 2018)

### 8.3 The 1980s "Deco Revival"

The 1980s saw a full-blown Art Deco revival in design, fashion, and popular culture:

- **Fashion**: Designers like **Giorgio Armani**, **Yves Saint Laurent**, and **Christian Lacroix** revived Deco shapes, beading, and geometric patterns
- **Film and TV**: *The Great Gatsby* (1974) — costumes by Theoni V. Aldredge; *Titanic* (1997) used Deco interiors for first-class spaces; *The Artist* (2011) revived the 1920s aesthetic
- **Interiors and furniture**: Reproduction Deco furniture, lighting, and accessories flooded the market
- **Music**: The electro-swing and big-band revival of the 1990s–2000s
- **Graphic design**: The 1980s "Memphis" movement (Ettore Sottsass, 1981) revived bold geometric patterns and bright colors, reinterpreting Deco for a postmodern age

### 8.4 Contemporary Revival (2000s–Present)

Art Deco continues to influence contemporary design:

- **Film**: *The Great Gatsby* (2013, Baz Luhrmann) — a CGI-enhanced hyper-Decor aesthetic; *Midnight in Paris* (2011, Woody Allen); *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (2014, Wes Anderson)
- **Fashion**: **Prada**, **Gucci**, **Tom Ford**, and **Ralph Lauren** regularly reference Art Deco in collections
- **Architecture**: Neo-Art Deco skyscrapers have appeared in many cities — the **Hearst Tower** (New York, 2006, Foster + Partners), the **Baccarat Hotel** (New York, 2015), and **8 Spruce Street** (New York, 2011, Frank Gehry) — sometimes in dialogue with historic Deco buildings
- **Interior design**: The "Deco Glam" trend in luxury hotels, bars, and restaurants (e.g., **Claridge's Bar** in London, **The NoMad** hotels, **Bar Hemingway** at the Ritz Paris)
- **Graphic design and branding**: Many luxury brands use Art Deco typography and geometric patterns for a vintage-luxury effect

### 8.5 Scholarship and Museums

The revival has been accompanied by sustained scholarly attention:

- Major exhibitions: *Art Deco 1910–1939* (V&A Museum, London, 2003); *The Birth of Art Deco: Ruhlmann and the Hôtel du Collectionneur* (Montreal Museum of Fine Arts, 2025); *1925–2025: Cent ans d'Art déco* (Musée des Arts Décoratifs, Paris, 2025–2026)
- Academic centers: The **Art Deco Society of New York**; the **Miami Design Preservation League**; the **Art Deco Trust** in Napier, New Zealand
- 2025 marked the **centenary of the 1925 Paris Exposition**, triggering a global wave of exhibitions, publications, and conferences celebrating Art Deco
